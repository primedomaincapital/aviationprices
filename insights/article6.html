Private Jet Sales Price Guide 2026: What Business Jets Actually Sell For

A used Citation CJ4 Gen2 sold in 2026 for more than its new list price. From a $3.5 million Cirrus Vision Jet to an $85 million Global 8000, here is every 2026 business-jet price that can actually be verified — and the five numbers that move any deal.

In September 2026, a two-year-old Cessna Citation CJ4 Gen2 sold for $12.5 million — more than Textron’s fully equipped list price for a new one. The same month, seven-year-old Embraer Phenom 300Es were listed at $13.75 million and $14.75 million, against a 2023 new list price of $10.995 million. In any other market, a used asset selling above its new price would be a misprint. In business aviation in 2026, it is the market: too few young, low-time aircraft for sale, order books stretched years out, and buyers who would rather pay a premium today than wait for a delivery slot in 2028.

That inversion is the first thing a price guide has to get right, because it breaks the assumption most buyers bring to the table — that the number on the sticker is the ceiling. It is not. It is a starting point, and in 2026 it is sometimes the floor. The second thing a guide has to get right is harder: most business-jet prices are not published at all. Gulfstream, Bombardier, and Dassault do not issue price lists. Embraer does not either. What exists instead is one comparable series — the "typically equipped" price in Aviation Week's Business & Commercial Aviation 2026 Purchase Planning Handbook, as published by cost-model provider FlyCraft — plus the valuation ranges that brokers and appraisers actually trade against. This guide is built only from those verifiable figures. Where no verified price exists, it says so.

How to read a jet price

Three distinctions decide whether a number is useful or misleading. First, new against pre-owned: a new price is a typically equipped figure — a green aircraft plus the standard completion, before options, custom interiors, connectivity, and the delivery slot itself, all of which push the real transaction higher. A pre-owned price is a range, because two aircraft of the same model and year can sit millions apart on hours, engine time, and maintenance status. Second, list against equipped: bare list prices run lower than typically equipped ones. GlobalAir lists the HondaJet Elite II at $6.95 million on a Q2 2025 factory basis; the 2026 typically equipped figure is $7.6 million. The Phenom 300E's Q2 2025 factory list was $10.995 million; typically equipped in 2026, it is $13.4 million. Compare list to list, or equipped to equipped, and never mix the two. Third, asking against transacted: an asking price is a hope with a tail number attached. A Vref value range, published by Duncan Aviation from Aircraft Value Reference data, is the industry's working estimate of what the aircraft is worth. A reported sale is the only number that is final — and reported sales are rare, because most change hands in private.

With those rules in place, the market reads as follows.

New jets, 2026: very light and light

The entry to the market costs less than most buyers expect and more than the brochures admit. Four jets in production cost under $10 million typically equipped in 2026, and the cheapest is a single-engine aircraft built for an owner-pilot. At the top of the light class, the bill has roughly quadrupled — and the aircraft is a different machine entirely.

The arithmetic at this end repays a second look. The G800 flies 8,200 nm — the longest range of any purpose-built business jet — for $4.5 million less than the G700, which flies 450 nm less. The Global 8000, delivered first on December 8, 2025, is the most expensive purpose-built business jet on sale at $85 million; Bombardier quoted $78 million when it launched the jet in 2022. The Falcon 6X, which entered service in November 2023, is the cheapest way into a new large-cabin Dassault at $59.4 million — and the Falcon 10X, which first flew in mid-2026, has no verified price at all. Dassault expects it to enter service in 2029. Any 10X price quoted before then is speculation.

The turboprop alternative

For buyers whose missions are regional, the jet is not the only answer, and the price gap is wide enough to matter. A Pilatus PC-12 NGX lists new at about $5.85 million to $6.2 million — the lower figure from BoltFlight's 2026 cost analysis, the upper from Aircraft Bluebook via Acejet — for a single-engine turboprop that carries up to nine passengers about 1,800 nm and lands on runways a jet cannot use. A Beechcraft King Air 260 runs about $7.78 million new, and the larger King Air 360 about $9.26 million, per BoltFlight's 2026 figures. The pre-owned side is cheaper still: Aircraft Bluebook pegs a 2008-model PC-12 at $3.95 million and a 2020-model at $5.75 million. The trade is speed and altitude against purchase price and runway access, and for a buyer flying 500-mile legs from a short field, it is a trade worth pricing honestly rather than dismissing as a lesser aircraft.

Pre-owned: where the real market lives

Most business jets change hands used, and the pre-owned market in 2026 is defined by scarcity at the young end. JETNET's mid-2026 data put just 6.6% of the fleet for sale, against a pre-pandemic norm of 9–12%. Average days on market reached 98, up 5.6% year on year. The telling number: 74% of the aircraft for sale are 16 years or older, up from 60% in 2019. The inventory that exists is largely aging airframes; young, low-time aircraft barely reach the market. That is why a two-year-old CJ4 Gen2 sold above list, and why the pre-owned tables below are worth more attention than the new ones.

Asking prices tell the same story from the other side of the trade. FlyCraft's listing estimates, checked October 5, 2026, put a 1970s Learjet 35A at $525,000 to $960,000, a Cessna Citation I at $549,000 to $1.3 million, a Citation II at $692,000 to $1 million, and a Learjet 31A at $770,000 to $980,000. The Phenom 300, the world's best-selling light jet for 14 years running, is the most documented pre-owned market of all: FlyCraft's Radar value model puts a 2010 aircraft at about $4.6 million and a 2023 at $13 million, with six source-reported sales between $7.3 million and $15.3 million in 2026. At the large end, Acejet's early-2026 market screen put a pre-owned Gulfstream G550 at $12 million to $22 million, a G650 at $30 million to $45 million with the G650ER adding $5 million to $10 million over a comparable G650, a Falcon 7X at $18 million to $28 million, a Falcon 8X at $35 million to $55 million, and a Global 7500 above $45 million pre-owned against above $70 million new.

The cheapest jets are the oldest, and the purchase price is the smallest bill. A $700,000 airframe does not come with $700,000 operating costs: crew salaries, hangar, and insurance are essentially the same whatever was paid, and maintenance and downtime are worse. The market's direction is itself contested — JETNET recorded 1,115 whole-aircraft transactions in the first half of 2026, down 10.8% year on year, while AMSTAT reported second-quarter 2026 business jet transactions up 15.5%, with heavy jets up 43.8%. Different periods, different methods; both agree that asking prices are falling fastest at the large-cabin end. Treat any single headline about the direction of the market with that disagreement in mind.

What moves a price up or down

Five factors do most of the work, and a buyer who prices them before the negotiation starts is the one who does not overpay.

Hours and cycles. Total airframe time and engine time set the baseline. A low-time aircraft of the same year commands a premium that can run into seven figures; a high-time one discounts steeply, because the next heavy inspection arrives sooner.

Engine program enrollment. JSSI, Rolls-Royce CorporateCare, Pratt & Whitney ESP, and Honeywell MSP do not publish rates — they are quoted per tail. The only publicly posted rates in the market are Pilatus CrystalCare: $4,799 a month plus $380 an hour for a PC-12, and $17,226 a month plus $820 an hour for a PC-24. An aircraft off program is worth less, and anyone quoting a generic program rate is guessing. Off program, budget $1 million or more per engine for overhaul on a large-cabin jet.

Logbooks and damage history. A pre-purchase inspection runs $15,000 to $50,000 for a business jet and takes two to four weeks end to end. Skip it and the exposure is larger than the fee: missing logbooks alone can cut appraised value by 10–20%, and up to 30% on complex aircraft. Damage history, even repaired, discounts a price for the life of the airframe.

Avionics and interior. A dated flight deck or a tired cabin is not a cosmetic problem; it is a six-figure refurbishment and weeks out of service. Budget $1.2 million to $2 million for paint and interior every seven to nine years. The reverse also holds: a fresh interior and current avionics are the cheapest premium a seller can buy.

Tax and fees around the purchase. Acquisition fees run 3–5% of purchase price for a broker or acquisition consultant on larger aircraft. Sales and use tax is the largest avoidable line: on a $20 million midsize jet, California's combined rates of roughly 7.25% to 11.25% mean $1.45 million to $2.25 million, while North Carolina caps aircraft tax at a flat $2,500 regardless of price, and Delaware, New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Oregon, and Rhode Island levy none. And the tax treatment changed in 2025: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying aircraft placed in service on or after January 20, 2025 — which supersedes the phased-down schedule still quoted in much published content, including this site's own earlier article on fractional costs. None of this is tax advice; the interaction with entity structure, state tax, and the 50% business-use rule is genuinely complicated, and an aviation tax specialist should be engaged before signing.

New against pre-owned: the depreciation inversion

The conventional wisdom is that a used jet saves 30–50% against new. In 2026 that is true for old metal and emphatically untrue for anything young and in demand. A seven-year-old Phenom 300E asking $14.75 million against a $10.995 million new list is not an anomaly to explain away; it is the price of immediacy when the factory queue is years long and the pre-owned shelf is nearly empty of young aircraft. Depreciation still runs 5–10% a year on most types, and the 74% of listings that are 16 years or older trade at the deep discounts the rule predicts. The inversion sits on top of that trend, not instead of it — a premium paid for the aircraft that can be flown next month rather than the one that can be ordered for 2028.

That premium is the counterargument to this entire guide, and it deserves to be stated plainly. A price guide implies that prices are knowable, stable, and comparable. In business aviation, none of the three is fully true. Typically equipped prices are planning values, not factory quotations; manufacturers negotiate each aircraft individually, and options, cabin choices, and delivery slots move the final number in both directions. Two of the most careful trackers in the industry disagree about whether transactions are rising or falling. The Falcon 10X has no verified price, the Gulfstream G400's entry-into-service date is open — General Dynamics chief executive Phebe Novakovic has said the company is "no longer in the business of estimating EIS, given that the regulators set the pace" — and the best data on pre-owned values, Vref, is a marketing value rather than an appraisal. A guide that hides those limits would be more precise and less honest. The numbers above are the best verified figures available in November 2026. They are a place to start a negotiation, not a substitute for one.

What the numbers mean for a buyer

The market underneath the guide is strong and getting stronger. GAMA reported 383 business jet deliveries in the first half of 2026, up 8.2%, with total airplane billings of $14.3 billion, up 15.5%. Order books are deep: Bombardier's backlog reached $21.8 billion at the end of the second quarter of 2026 and Gulfstream's $24.0 billion, with both targeting around 157–160 deliveries for the year. Honeywell's October 2025 Global Business Aviation Outlook projects 8,500 new business jet deliveries valued at $283 billion over the next decade, the highest forecast in the report's history, with 91% of operators surveyed saying they plan to fly the same or more in 2026. Demand is not the constraint. Young supply is.

For a buyer, that leaves a short list of decisions that matter more than the sticker price. Buy the mission before the model: the range, runway, and passenger count that fit the actual flying, not the aircraft that impresses at the ramp. Price against two series, not one — the BCA typically equipped figure for new, the Vref range for pre-owned — and treat any number that appears in only one of them as unverified. Pay for the pre-purchase inspection before the deposit becomes non-refundable; $15,000 to $50,000 is the cheapest insurance in the transaction. And if the aircraft is young, low-time, and on an engine program, expect to pay for that combination — in 2026, the market is paying above list for exactly that aircraft, and the buyer who waits for a discount on it is waiting for a market that no longer exists.
