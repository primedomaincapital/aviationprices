What Does a Private Jet Actually Cost Per Hour in 2026?

The same Phenom 300 costs about $2,315 an hour to operate, $3,500 to $5,500 an hour to charter, and $8,600 an hour on a NetJets card. Fuel, crew, and maintenance reserves explain the gap — and the quoted rate is rarely the billed one.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

In the spring of 2026, the chief financial officer of a mid-sized logistics company asked her broker a simple question: what does a Phenom 300 cost per hour? She got three answers. The broker's operating-cost model said $2,315. A charter quote said $4,200. The NetJets card rate said $8,600. Same aircraft, same hour, three prices — and every one of them was honest.

The confusion is not a sales trick. It is the structure of the market. A private jet hour is priced three different ways because the seller is selling three different things: the cost of running the machine, the cost of renting it for a trip, and the cost of guaranteed access to it on demand. The buyer who compares those three numbers as if they were one product will conclude that someone is lying. Nobody is.

Three prices for the same hour

Start with the cheapest number, because it is the one most often quoted out of context. The variable cost of flying a Phenom 300 — fuel, maintenance reserves, landing and handling fees — runs $2,315 to $2,543 an hour, according to a 2026 budget breakdown published by Manext, with fuel alone contributing $1,127 to $1,149 at Jet-A prices of $6.00 to $7.23 a gallon. That is the cost of one more hour in the air, once the aircraft, the crew, and the hangar are already paid for. It is a real number — and the least useful one a buyer can carry into a negotiation, because it excludes everything that does not scale with flight time.

FlyCraft's operating-cost model, updated October 5, 2026, prices the same aircraft honestly: $501,324 a year in fixed costs — crew, training, hangar, insurance, management — plus $3,315 an hour in variable costs, for a total of $1,164,324 a year at 200 hours. That works out to $5,822 an hour, all in. The fixed block is what turns a $2,315 variable hour into a $5,822 real hour: at 200 hours a year, the crew and the hangar cost $2,507 for every hour flown, whether the aircraft is in the air or sitting in it.

Charter is the second price, and it is higher because the operator has to cover the same fixed and variable costs, plus positioning the aircraft to the departure airport, plus margin, plus the 7.5% federal excise tax on domestic flights. Private Jet Price List's October 2026 estimates put light-jet charter at $3,500 to $5,500 an hour before tax and fees. BizJet Journal's published 2026 rate card is tighter — light jets $3,750 to $4,600 — and Approved Jets, aggregating broker quotes, puts the market average for charter at about $5,800 a flight hour across all categories.

The third price is the jet card, and it is the most expensive because it sells certainty. NetJets, owned by Berkshire Hathaway since 1998, charges $215,000 for a 25-hour card on a Phenom 300 — $8,600 per occupied hour, federal excise tax included — as this site's earlier analysis reported. Flexjet prices its 25-hour Phenom 300 card at roughly $7,937 an occupied hour, and Wheels Up caps light-jet rates from about $7,395 an hour on a deposit-funded membership. The card buyer is not paying for the hour. The buyer is paying for a jet that arrives when the phone rings, with no repositioning fee and no availability argument. That guarantee is the product, and the premium over charter is the price of it.

The charter table, and why three publishers disagree

Published charter rates for 2026 cluster in the same bands but do not agree, and the disagreement is instructive. Private Jet Price List, as of October 9, 2026, estimates turboprops at $1,800 to $3,200 an hour, very light jets at $2,800 to $4,200, light jets at $3,500 to $5,500, midsize at $4,500 to $7,000, super-midsize at $6,500 to $9,500, large-cabin at $8,500 to $13,000, and ultra-long-range at $12,000 to $20,000 or more. BizJet Journal's 2026 rate card runs lower at every tier: light jets $3,750 to $4,600, midsize $4,700 to $5,500, super-midsize $6,200 to $7,500, large jets $8,500 to $10,000, ultra-long-range $12,000 to $17,000. A third measure, the Travel Statistics Bureau's mid-2026 reported averages cited by Approved Jets, sits above both: $8,456 an hour for light jets, $9,557 for midsize, and $15,236 for large-cabin jets.

Those averages are not a contradiction of the rate cards. They are a different measurement: a rate card quotes the aircraft, while an average of completed trips includes the repositioning, taxes, fees, and peak-day pricing the rate card leaves out. Approved Jets frames the averages as a higher-side budgeting check: plan against the average, negotiate against the rate card, and treat any quote far below both as a question rather than a bargain.

What an hour is made of

Fuel is the largest variable line and the most volatile. The U.S. Gulf Coast wholesale spot price was $3.76 a gallon on November 17, 2026, according to the Energy Information Administration, as published by FlyCraft. What a private jet pays at an airport fixed-base operator is roughly double: FlyCraft estimates about $7.12 a gallon at the FBO, and AirNav's nationwide survey of 2,596 FBOs averaged $7.66 on October 4, 2026. The Aviation Research Group's survey, reported by Aviation Week, put the August 2026 average higher still at $8.31 across more than 200 U.S. FBOs, up $1.70 on a year earlier, with the West at $9.09 against $7.36 in the Central region.

How much it burns depends on size. FlyCraft's fuel model puts the median large jet at 329 gallons an hour — $2,342 an hour at FBO prices — and the median ultra-long-range jet at 458 gallons an hour, or $3,261. Each $1 move in the price of a gallon adds $329 an hour to a large jet's fuel bill and $458 to an ultra-long-range jet's. A Phenom 300 burns roughly 170 to 183 gallons an hour. A Gulfstream G650 burns roughly 498. That spread is the main reason the hourly rate doubles and triples as the cabin grows.

Crew is the largest fixed line, and it is climbing faster than any other. The National Business Aviation Association's 2026 Compensation Survey, released August 18 and covering 352 member organizations and 4,090 employees, found salaries up nearly 7% in some job categories. The prior year's survey found bonuses adding an average 13% on top of salary, pilots on ultra-long-range aircraft paid more than 36% above the average, and pilots with ten or more years at the same company earning 22% more than average. In dollars, a midsize-jet captain earns $150,000 to $220,000 a year, according to Private Jet Nation's 2026 breakdown, with recurrent simulator training for two pilots adding $50,000 to $90,000; a captain on a Gulfstream G700 or G800 commands $330,000 to $420,000. Spread a two-pilot crew across 200 hours a year and the crew alone costs $1,500 to $2,500 an hour.

Maintenance reserves are the line buyers most often underestimate, because they are invisible until they are not. Engine programs — JSSI, Rolls-Royce CorporateCare, Pratt & Whitney ESP, Honeywell MSP — charge a monthly fee plus an hourly rate, quoted per tail. The only publicly posted program rates in the market, as this site's price guide reported, are Pilatus CrystalCare: $4,799 a month plus $380 an hour for a PC-12, and $17,226 a month plus $820 an hour for a PC-24. Off program, a large-cabin engine overhaul can cost $1 million or more per engine. The reserve is not a fee for service. It is a savings account for the day the engine comes off the wing.

Why the quote is not the invoice

The hourly rate is the headline. The invoice is the story. Approved Jets puts the gap at 20% to 40%: the all-in trip cost typically runs a fifth to two-fifths above the advertised hourly rate once positioning, taxes, and crew charges are added. A two-hour light-jet flight priced at $4,500 to $6,500 an hour becomes an invoice of approximately $11,000 to $15,000.

Repositioning is the largest addition: the aircraft rarely sits at the departure airport, so it flies empty to collect the passengers, and the buyer pays for those empty hours. Federal excise tax adds 7.5% on domestic charter, plus a segment tax of $5.30 per passenger per segment in 2026, per the IRS schedule cited by BizJet Journal. Landing and handling fees can run into four figures at congested fields, and a crew overnight adds hotel and per diem. None of these is hidden. All of them are absent from the hourly rate, because the hourly rate prices the aircraft, not the trip.

Minimums do the same work in the other direction. Most operators bill a daily minimum, so a 45-minute hop is charged as a longer flight, and a one-way booking can carry the cost of the empty return. The mechanism is legitimate — the operator's costs do not shrink because the passenger's meeting ended early — but the effective hourly rate on a short trip can be double the quoted rate.

The hourly rate prices the aircraft. The invoice prices the trip. The gap between them is where the money is.

The case against the hourly rate

The hourly rate is arguably the wrong unit of analysis entirely, and the argument starts with the fixed block. An owner who flies 100 hours a year pays far more per hour than an owner who flies 400, on the same aircraft, with the same crew. FlyCraft's model makes the arithmetic plain for the Phenom 300: fixed costs of $501,324 spread over 100 hours add $5,013 to every hour, on top of $3,315 in variable costs, for an all-in rate of about $8,328 an hour. At 400 hours, the fixed share falls to $1,253 and the all-in rate to about $4,568.

That arithmetic is also the honest case for not owning at all. A flyer under roughly 25 hours a year is almost always better off chartering: no prepayment, no commitment, no fixed block to amortize. The jet card exists for the 25-to-50-hour customer who wants availability without an asset, and the card's premium is the price of that middle ground. The cheapest hour in private aviation is not the one with the lowest rate. It is the one flown by the buyer whose hours match the product they bought.

The hourly rate also flatters the wrong comparison. A light jet at $4,000 an hour that needs a fuel stop on a 2,000-mile mission is not cheaper than a super-midsize jet at $7,000 an hour that flies it nonstop, once the extra landing fees, crew time, and lost afternoon are counted. Range, runway, and passenger count are part of the price, and they do not appear in it.

What the numbers mean for a buyer

The practical rule is simple and almost never followed: never compare hourly rates. Compare all-in trip prices under stated assumptions — same route, same dates, same passenger count, same return. Ask every operator for the same three numbers: the occupied hourly rate, the repositioning charge, and the total with tax. The operator that will not give all three is telling you something about the fourth number, the one that arrives on the invoice.

For the buyer deciding between charter, card, and ownership, the hours decide. Under 25 hours a year, charter wins on the math and on flexibility. Between 25 and 50, the card buys availability at a premium worth it only if the flying is time-critical. Above 150 to 200 hours, ownership's fixed block starts to pay for itself — and the per-hour figure the owner quotes afterward will look suspiciously low, because it will be the variable number, not the real one. The market has three prices for the same hour because it has three different buyers. The expensive mistake is buying the wrong one.
