How Long Does It Take to Buy a Private Jet?

A pre-owned jet usually takes 30 to 90 days from accepted offer to closing — and three to nine months from first search to delivery. The pre-buy inspection, the title search, and the financing clock decide which end of that range you land on.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The deal was supposed to take thirty days. A midsize jet, $6.5 million asking price, negotiated to $5.9 million. The letter of intent was signed, the deposit posted, the inspection facility booked. Then the logbooks arrived — part digital, part handwritten, with airworthiness directives that did not line up. The records review stretched from one week to two and a half. Ninety days later the parties were still looking for a path forward. Four months after that, the deal closed, with lawsuits following. Jason Zilberbrand, president of valuation firm VREF, tells the story on his firm's podcast as the deal from hell, and his moral is blunt: time kills deals.

The story is extreme, but the mechanism is ordinary. Buying a private jet is not slow because the paperwork is long. It is slow because the transaction has a dozen independent clocks running at once — the inspection facility's backlog, the lender's underwriting, the title company's lien search, the FAA's registration queue — and the closing happens only when the slowest one finishes.

The honest answer

For a pre-owned jet, the published ranges converge on a window of roughly one to four months from offer to delivery, with the spread depending on where the clock starts. Jet Linx puts it at 30 to 90 days from an accepted offer to closing, and three to nine months from first search to delivery. P3 Jets puts the typical transaction at 60 to 120 days from signed letter of intent to delivery. Sky Travel gives the same 60-to-120-day range from engagement to delivery. Athena Jets puts a straightforward pre-owned acquisition at two to four months from mandate to delivery. Safe Fly's buyer's guide gives 30 to 90 days as the total typical acquisition period.

Those numbers agree more than they appear to, because they measure different spans. The search — defining the mission, shortlisting aircraft, viewing them — takes two to six weeks on its own, and it runs before any clock the seller cares about starts. A buyer who counts from the first broker call and a buyer who counts from the signed letter of intent will report timelines that differ by a month or more for the same transaction, and both will be right.

New aircraft are a different market with a different calendar. Jet Linx puts a new order at six to 18 months or longer, depending on the build slot and customization; Jettly puts new orders at 12 to 24 months. Both understate the wait for the most sought-after models, because the factory queue is the constraint. The General Aviation Manufacturers Association reported 383 business jet deliveries in the first half of 2026, up 8.2% on the year — yet Bombardier's order backlog reached $21.8 billion at the end of the second quarter and Gulfstream's $24.0 billion, as this site's price guide reported. Demand is not the constraint. Young supply is. A buyer ordering a new ultra-long-range jet in 2026 is joining a queue measured in years, and the pre-owned premium for young, low-time aircraft — sometimes above new list price — is the market pricing exactly that wait.

The timeline, phase by phase

The acquisition breaks into seven phases, each with its own clock. The search takes one to four weeks, according to Safe Fly's guide, or two to six weeks by the brokerage estimates — longer when the mission is unusual, shorter when the buyer knows the model. The letter of intent and deposit take about a week: the buyer signs and posts a deposit commonly set at 5% to 10% of the purchase price into escrow, refundable until technical acceptance, after which it goes hard, in VREF's account of the standard flow.

The pre-purchase inspection is the longest single phase. Jetcraft, one of the largest aircraft dealers, puts it at 10 to 20 working days, depending on the aircraft's age, history, and records. Athena Jets and Safe Fly give two to four weeks. P3 Jets, allowing for facility backlog and scope, gives two to six weeks. The inspection itself is only part of the time: the aircraft has to be ferried to the facility, the facility has to have a slot, and the discrepancy list the facility issues has to be corrected or negotiated before the buyer signs the technical acceptance. A clean aircraft on a quiet schedule can clear in two weeks. A 20-year-old aircraft with incomplete records cannot.

Financing runs on a parallel clock of about 30 days, according to The Private Jet Company, which completes roughly 40 aircraft transactions a year, about 80% of them acquisitions. The lender needs the same inspection the buyer does, plus an appraisal, plus the title search — which is why financing started late is financing that delays the closing. Legal work, escrow, and registration run one to three weeks from technical acceptance to delivery, in Athena Jets' table, and the FAA's own processing adds 16 to 20 working days for a standard registration, according to the figures cited by registration advisory firm ValiAir. The registration queue does not stop the buyer from flying: the pink copy of the registration application serves as temporary authority to operate within the United States while the certificate is issued, and the FAA's CARES electronic system, in place since January 2025, accepts electronic documents and can issue electronic certificates.

The inspection is the clock

Every source agrees on one point: the pre-buy inspection is where the timeline is made or lost, and where the money is saved or wasted. The cost alone is a range with a spread wide enough to matter. This site's price guide put a pre-purchase inspection at $15,000 to $50,000 for a business jet. The Private Jet Company puts the range at $5,000 to $80,000. Jettly puts it at $50,000 to $100,000. The three ranges overlap but do not agree, because the scope is not standardized: a records-and-visual review costs a fraction of a full inspection at the interval with borescopes, gear function checks, and systems testing, and the aircraft's size and age move the number in both directions.

What the inspection finds moves the price more than the inspection costs. Jettly reports that findings can adjust the price by 5% to 15% or require repairs — on a $10 million aircraft, that is $500,000 to $1.5 million, against a fee measured in tens of thousands. Missing logbooks alone can cut appraised value by 10% to 20%, and up to 30% on complex aircraft, as this site's price guide reported. Damage history, even repaired, discounts a price for the life of the airframe. The buyer pays for the inspection; the seller pays to correct airworthiness discrepancies found during it, in P3 Jets' account of the standard allocation. The inspection is the cheapest insurance in the transaction — and the phase most likely to extend it, because a facility that finds corrosion, an overdue inspection, or an engine approaching overhaul does not hand the aircraft back the next morning.

What actually delays closings

The single biggest cause of delayed closings is not the inspection. It is the title. Aircraft titles do not work like car titles: there is no state certificate, and ownership and liens are recorded federally with the FAA Aircraft Registry in Oklahoma City. The most common problem title companies see, according to Cash4Planes' account of the escrow process, is a loan paid off years ago whose release was never recorded. It is fixable — the title company contacts the lender or its successor for a release — but tracking down a release from a lender that has merged or closed can take days or weeks. Some aviation lenders take five to ten business days just to issue a payoff letter.

The VREF case shows how the delays compound. The logbook review dragged from one week to two and a half. The escrow froze funds during a dispute over default. The maintenance facility held the aircraft while engine swaps and airframe modifications were required. Ninety days went by just finding a path forward. The transaction planned for thirty days took four months to complete, and the aircraft never reached Part 135 conformity for charter. The lesson Zilberbrand draws is procedural, not moral: order a title and lien search before going to market, have the entity, financing, and insurance ready before making an offer, and read the escrow instructions rather than assuming they protect you.

Other delays are quieter. An aircraft previously registered in a foreign country needs deregistration there before FAA registration here, and import registrations carry their own queue. A buyer financing the purchase needs the lender's funding commitment before the deposit goes hard, and the lender needs the appraisal and the inspection first. Tax and entity structuring — the owning entity, the state of registration, the 50% business-use rule — takes weeks when done properly and months when started late. The 2025 restoration of 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying aircraft placed in service on or after January 20, 2025, which this site's price guide reported, made the structuring question more valuable and no faster to answer. None of these steps is difficult. All of them are sequential, and sequential steps are what make a 30-day plan into a 120-day transaction.

The fastest closings are not the ones with the fewest steps. They are the ones where the slow steps started first.

The case for going fast

Speed is possible, and sellers reward it. The Private Jet Company reports an average transaction of two to six months — and a record of five days from letter of intent to closing. A five-day closing requires an aircraft with clean records, a buyer with financing and insurance already arranged, a seller with nothing to hide, and an escrow agent who has done it before. It is real, and it is not a plan.

The counterargument to the slow, careful timeline is that delay has its own cost. Aircraft get sold to faster buyers. Inspection slots disappear. A seller who has waited ninety days for a buyer to finish diligence starts to wonder whether the buyer is serious, and the next offer starts to look better. VREF's warning — time kills deals — cuts both ways: the buyer who rushes buys someone else's problem, and the buyer who dawdles loses the aircraft to someone who did not. The timeline is a negotiation, not a schedule, and the buyer who controls it is the buyer who started the slow work before the offer was made.

What the numbers mean for a buyer

Plan on 60 to 120 days from letter of intent to delivery for a pre-owned jet, and three to nine months from the first broker call. Budget the inspection at $15,000 to $50,000 for a typical business jet, and treat a quote outside that band as a question about scope. Order the title and lien search before the inspection, not after it — the unreleased lien is the delay that no amount of inspection speed can fix, and it costs nothing to check early. Have financing, insurance, and the owning entity arranged before the offer, not during the inspection, because the lender's 30-day clock and the inspector's two-to-six-week clock run in parallel only if both start on time.

And plan the weeks after closing in parallel with the weeks before it. P3 Jets' advice is the most practical in the acquisition literature: crew hiring or transition training, insurance binding, the management agreement, program enrollments, hangar space, and the first scheduled inspection all land in the same few weeks after delivery, and buyers who plan them during the inspection fly weeks earlier than buyers who start after closing. Delivery is not the finish line. The first flight is, and the first flight is scheduled during the inspection, not after it.
