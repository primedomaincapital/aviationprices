Jet Fuel Surcharges and Positioning Fees: The Charges Buried in the Charter Quote

The quote says $8,000 an hour. The invoice says something else. Positioning, fuel surcharges, landing fees, de-icing, and the 7.5% federal tax that applies to all of it — the line items to negotiate before you book.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The quote was $8,000 an hour for a light jet, four hours, $32,000. The invoice was $47,000. The difference was not a mistake. It was the rest of the price, itemized on the second page, in the section of the quote the customer had not read — or had read and not understood. Positioning. Fuel surcharge. Landing fees. Federal excise tax. De-icing, which nobody can quote in advance. The hourly rate is the number the broker leads with, and it is the least informative number in the transaction. The real price of a charter is the hourly rate plus everything else, and everything else is 30 to 50 percent of the total.

Positioning is the largest of the buried charges, and it is the one that surprises first-time charterers most. A jet does not live where you do. It lives where its owner bases it, and getting it to your departure airport is a flight — flown empty, billed at the standard hourly rate, and counted in the total. Positioning costs $3,000 to $15,000 or more depending on the distance and the aircraft, and it is billed as additional flight hours, which means a four-hour trip with a two-hour positioning leg is a six-hour invoice. The positioning leg is also taxable: the 7.5 percent federal excise tax on domestic charter applies to the positioning flight as well as the passenger flight, because the tax applies to the transportation, not to the passengers. A customer who negotiates the hourly rate down by $500 and ignores the positioning leg has saved $2,000 and left $10,000 on the table.

The fuel surcharge, and why it is not optional

Fuel is the largest variable cost in a charter, and the surcharge is how the operator passes it through. Jet fuel costs about $3.99 a gallon in 2026, and fuel can account for 30 to 40 percent of the total charter cost on a long-range flight. The surcharge is typically 10 to 15 percent over the base hourly rate, and it is set by the operator, not the market — which means it varies between operators, and it is negotiable, and it is the line item most customers never ask about. The surcharge is also the line item that moves: when fuel prices rise, the surcharge rises, and the quote you received last month is not the quote you will receive this month. Some operators build the fuel cost into the hourly rate and charge no separate surcharge. Some charge both. The question to ask is not whether there is a surcharge. It is what the all-in hourly rate is, including fuel, and whether the surcharge is fixed for the booking or floats with the fuel index.

The federal taxes are the charges nobody negotiates, because nobody can. The federal excise tax on domestic charter is 7.5 percent, applied to the total amount paid for the transportation — including the positioning leg, the fuel surcharge, and any other charge that is part of the transportation. It is non-negotiable, and it is the operator's obligation to collect and remit it, which means it appears on the invoice whether or not the customer expected it. The segment fee is $5.30 per passenger per leg in 2026, a small number that adds up on a multi-leg trip with a full cabin. The international head tax is about $23.40 per passenger on international departures. None of these are large individually. Together they add 8 to 10 percent to the invoice, and they are the reason the quote and the invoice never match.

Landing fees, de-icing, and the rest

Landing fees run $500 to $3,000 or more at major hubs, and they are set by the airport, not the operator — which means they are not negotiable, and they are the reason a departure from Teterboro costs more than a departure from a regional field. The fees are higher at congested airports, higher at peak hours, and higher for larger aircraft, and they are charged per landing, which means a multi-leg trip pays them multiple times. De-icing is the charge that cannot be quoted in advance, because it depends on the weather: hundreds to thousands of dollars per application, applied when the conditions require it, with no way to know in advance whether it will be needed or how many applications the flight will require. A winter departure from Chicago can add $2,000 to $5,000 in de-icing to a trip that was quoted without it, and the customer discovers the charge when the invoice arrives.

Catering is the smallest of the buried charges and the most negotiable. A standard catering order — sandwiches, fruit, coffee — is often included in the base rate. A custom order — a specific vintage, a hot meal, a particular restaurant — is billed at cost plus a markup, and the markup is where the operator makes money. The customer who asks for the catering menu in advance, and who specifies exactly what they want, pays less than the customer who says "just handle it." The same principle applies to ground transportation, which is sometimes included and sometimes billed separately, and to overnight fees, which apply when the aircraft and crew stay overnight at the destination. The overnight fee is a per-day charge, and it is the line item that turns a day trip into a two-day invoice.

The counterargument is that the buried charges are not hidden — they are itemized, and a customer who reads the quote carefully will find them. That is true, and it is beside the point. The quote is written to be read by someone who charters regularly, and the first-time charterer does not know what positioning means, does not know that the fuel surcharge floats, and does not know that the 7.5 percent tax applies to the positioning leg. The industry's answer is the jet card, and it is a real answer: a NetJets card, at about $215,000 for a 25-hour block, charges no fuel surcharge, no positioning fee for flights within the service area, and a single all-in hourly rate. The card costs more per hour than an on-demand charter, and it costs less in surprises. For a customer who flies 25 hours a year and does not want to read a quote line by line, the card is the honest price. For a customer who flies occasionally and reads carefully, the on-demand charter is cheaper — if the customer asks the right questions before booking.

The questions are the same for every operator, and they should be asked in writing, before the deposit is paid. What is the positioning cost, and is it included in the quote? What is the fuel surcharge, and is it fixed or floating? What are the landing fees at each airport on the itinerary? Is de-icing included, or billed at cost? What is the cancellation policy — because a cancellation within 24 to 48 hours of departure is charged at 50 to 100 percent of the total, and the total includes the positioning, the fuel, and the tax. And what is the all-in price, in a single number, for the trip as flown? The operator who will not give a single all-in number is the operator whose quote you should not trust. The hourly rate is the advertisement. The all-in price is the product.
