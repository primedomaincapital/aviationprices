Fractional Flying Is Eating Private Aviation — and the Jet Card Is the Casualty

Business jet departures hit a record 3.88 million in 2025, and fractional flying grew faster than every other way to fly private. NetJets limited new jet card sales in July 2026. The industry’s middle product is being squeezed from both sides.

The quote said $4,500 per hour. The invoice said $22,000. The flight was from New York to Miami — roughly 2.5 hours each way. By the broker’s math, that should have been $22,500 for the round trip. Close enough. Except the invoice had line items the quote hadn’t mentioned: $3,200 in repositioning fees (the jet had to fly empty from Teterboro to pick them up), $1,650 in federal excise tax (7.5 percent on domestic flights), $800 in landing fees, $600 for crew overnight (the return was the next day), and $1,200 in catering that nobody remembered ordering.

The prospect wanted a jet card. Twenty-five hours, fixed rate, no ownership, no five-year commitment — the product the industry spent a decade selling as the sensible middle. The salesperson listened, nodded, and steered the conversation toward a share. The card, it turned out, was not quite available on the terms the prospect had read about. The share was. That conversation, repeated thousands of times in 2026, is the sound of private aviation reorganizing itself around the customer who commits — and quietly rationing the customer who will not.

The demand data says the reorganization is working. According to WINGX, global business jet departures reached 3,878,336 in 2025, an all-time record, up 4.6% on 2024 and 34% above pre-COVID 2019 levels. The summer of 2026 was the busiest on record: nearly 947,000 departures worldwide between June 1 and August 23, up 2.3% on summer 2025 and 35% above the same period in 2019, with North America accounting for more than 71% of global activity. ARGUS reported global flight activity up 3.4% year over year in June and 5.6% in July, with six consecutive positive months, and forecasts 2026 activity up approximately 1.6% on 2025’s record. The market is not just busy. It is busy at a structurally higher baseline than before the pandemic.

The numbers behind the shift

Fractional flying is growing faster than every other way to fly private. ARGUS data for June 2026 shows North American activity up 2.2%, led by fractional operations, which increased 10.4% year over year. Charter under Part 135 grew 2%. Full ownership under Part 91 fell 1.1%. The split is sharpest in large-cabin jets: the category fell 3.3% overall, including a 7.1% drop in Part 91 and a 3.4% drop in Part 135 — yet large-cabin fractional flying rose 13.3%. September told the same story, with North American activity up 0.9% and large-cabin fractional flying up 13.9%. Fractional fleets have grown over 65% since 2019, and Part 91K fractional departures were up 10.3% in the first half of 2025 alone, against 4.2% for charter. Honeywell’s October 2025 Global Business Aviation Outlook — the 34th annual edition — projects 8,500 new business jet deliveries valued at $283 billion over the next decade, the highest forecast in the report’s history, with 91% of operators surveyed saying they plan to fly the same or more in 2026.

The card, priced out

The jet card was supposed to be the answer for the 25-to-50-hour flyer. NetJets, owned by Berkshire Hathaway since 1998 and the world’s largest private jet company with roughly 800 aircraft, sells its 25-hour card at $215,000 — $8,600 per occupied hour on a Phenom 300 light jet, with the 7.5% federal excise tax included, on its Card275 program. A 1/16 share in a light jet runs about $500,000 to $900,000, plus a monthly management fee of about $10,000 to $30,000 and an hourly rate, bringing the year-one total to roughly $1.1 million to $1.3 million for 50 hours. In late July 2026, industry press reported that NetJets had limited new jet card sales. The card is the only rate NetJets publishes with any precision — and it is the product the company is now rationing.

The rivals show the same squeeze from the other side. Flexjet, with a fleet of roughly 300 aircraft, prices its 25-hour card on a Phenom 300 at roughly $7,937 per occupied hour in 2026, about $198,425 for the 25-hour minimum before fuel and tax. Wheels Up, the deposit-funded membership, charges $8,500 a year plus a $100,000 flight-fund deposit, with capped light-jet rates starting around $7,395 an hour. VistaJet sells its VJ25 program for 25 to 49 hours a year and its Program for 50 or more, on a three-year term, at quote-only rates estimated at $15,000 an hour or more — no ownership, no repositioning fees, 187 countries. Sentient Jet’s SJ25 light-jet card runs $183,100 for 25 hours. The spread between the cheapest card and the most expensive is nearly two-to-one, and none of the operators publishes a full rate sheet. The card buyer is shopping in the dark, and the operators know it.

Who the shift favors — and who it strands

The shift favors the committed flyer and the operator in equal measure. A fractional owner gets guaranteed availability, crew continuity, and an asset that can be sold back at the end of the term. The operator gets five years of contracted revenue and an aircraft that flies on a schedule it controls. The World Cup summer showed how that demand behaves under stress: WINGX recorded sharp surges at host-city airports as the tournament opened, with Seattle roughly doubling its normal daily traffic around its opening group match and Mexico City running about 1.7 times its baseline. Event demand is real, but the durable growth is fractional — customers purchasing lifts at healthy levels, month after month, rather than chartering for a weekend.

The case for the other side

There is a real argument that the fractional boom is a late-cycle story, and it starts with the crew roster. Business aviation is flying more than ever with crews that are already stretched: U.S. major airlines hired 648 pilots in August 2026, more than double May’s total, bringing 2026 hiring to 4,511 through August — already matching all of 2025. Jet fuel climbed above $200 a barrel after the closure of the Strait of Hormuz early in 2026, according to IATA data, and Middle East private flight activity fell by close to a third year over year at the height of the regional conflict. And the fractional commitment itself is the catch: a five-year share carries a 30% to 40% depreciation loss at exit, while a charter or a card costs nothing when the flying stops. For anyone under roughly 25 hours a year, charter still wins on the math — no prepayment, no commitment, and a market that ARGUS expects to keep growing through the rest of 2026.

The choice, then, is not between good and bad products. It is between a market that rewards commitment with availability and punishes hesitation with rationing, and a pay-as-you-go market that rewards flexibility with higher per-trip prices. The flyer who knows their hours should buy the share. The flyer who does not should charter — and should do it before the card they were counting on gets rationed again.
